# Student Site Design — Research Brief

**Colorful / gaming vs. GT-branded: what maximizes middle-school engagement, and how to avoid brand confusion.**
_Prepared for the Austin Speedrun design review._

---

## TL;DR

It's not "colorful vs. GT-branded", it's **match each design to its audience**.

- **Student page → keep the colorful/gaming look.** The users are 13-year-olds, and the engagement evidence is strong and consistent.
- **Parent page → keep the refined GT look.** The deciders are parents, and design is what drives their trust.
- **Resolve "don't confuse the programs" with an *endorsed sub-brand*:** "Austin Speedrun **by GT School**", carried by a consistent GT logo, an explicit endorsement line, and cross-links on both sites. This gives the sub-brand its own energy while transferring credibility from the parent brand.
- **Then confirm with your own A/B test** on Austin middle-schoolers. The studies below are strong priors; your metrics are the definitive answer.

---

## The four numbers that frame the decision

| Metric | Value | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Gamification effect on learning | **g = 0.82** (large; 41 studies, 5,071 learners) | Game framing measurably lifts learning/motivation for students |
| Warm, saturated color | **+42%** visual fixation (engagement β = 0.63) | Bold color captures and holds young attention |
| Gen Z dark-mode preference | **~73%** | Dark + bold reads as "for me / premium / gaming" to teens |
| Credibility from "design look" | **46.1%** of judgments (n = 2,684) | For parents, the look *is* the first trust test |

## Evidence — colorful / gaming aesthetic (audience: students)

- **Big learning effect.** Gamification shows a large overall effect on outcomes (g = 0.82; 41 studies, 5,071 learners), with motivation the biggest driver. — [Gamification meta-analysis (2023)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10591086/)
- **Match their world.** Game visuals tied to kids' interests raised intrinsic motivation versus generic / "corporate" badges. — [Badge-aesthetics experiment (2023)](https://doi.org/10.5753/sbie.2023.234313)
- **Warm color wins attention.** Red/orange grab attention fastest; saturated palettes lifted visual fixation +42% and engagement (β = 0.63). — [Color perception & engagement (2025)](https://doi.org/10.59324/ejahss.2025.2(6).16), [Gen Z color study](https://doi.org/10.1117/12.3088750)
- **Gen Z rejects "corporate."** This age group gravitates to bold, vibrant palettes; generic corporate colors underperform. — [Designing for Gen Z](https://atrainmarketing.com/blog/graphic-design/designing-for-gen-z/)
- **Dark mode is the default.** ~73% of Gen Z prefer dark mode; brands now treat it as an expectation, and it signals premium / gaming. — [YPulse (2026)](https://www.ypulse.com/newsfeed/2026/06/23/gen-z-demand-is-forcing-brands-to-include-dark-mode-features-on-their-apps/)

---

## Evidence — GT-branded / refined aesthetic (audience: parents & brand trust)

- **Design drives credibility.** "Design look" was the #1 cited factor in credibility judgments, present in 46.1% of assessments (n = 2,684). — [Stanford Web Credibility](https://credibility.stanford.edu/pdf/How_Do_People_Evaluate_a_Web_Site%27s_Credibility_v37.pdf)
- **Trust forms in ~50 ms.** Users judge a site almost instantly on visuals (the aesthetic-usability effect). — [Lindgaard et al. (2006)](https://www.makinggood.ac.nz/media/1265/lindegaardetal_2006_attention.pdf)
- **Meet category expectations.** Looking like a credible program of its type (prototypicality) is the strongest direct driver of trust (n = 1,530). — [Prototypicality & trust](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijhcs.2023.103103)
- **Coherence builds recall.** Visually cohesive brand portfolios boost recognition and recall (36% vs. 1% correct recall in one test). — [Visual branding cohesion](https://doi.org/10.1177/14707853241291443)
- **Confusion is the real risk.** Over-differentiated sub-brands erode coherence and trust, exactly the concern raised in the review. — [Brand portfolio coherence](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167811617300812)

---

## Recommendation — split by audience, unify by endorsement

Keep the **colorful/gaming look on the student page** (users are kids; the engagement evidence is strong and consistent) and the **refined GT look on the parent page** (deciders are parents; the trust evidence is strong).

Resolve the brand-confusion concern with an **endorsed sub-brand**: "Austin Speedrun **by GT School**", carried consistently by:

1. the **GT logo** present on both experiences,
2. an explicit **"by GT School"** endorsement line, and
3. **cross-links** between the student and parent sites.

This is textbook endorsed-brand architecture: independence and energy for the sub-brand, credibility transfer from the parent. — [Brand architecture guide](https://www.creativeans.com/resources/brand-architecture-for-multi-brand-companies-when-to-create-sub-brands-when-to-endorse-when-to-merge)

---

## Caveats and next step

- **Bold, not chaotic.** Use saturated color purposefully; too many competing themes can distract, a caution raised even within the pro-gamification research.
- **Dark mode needs care.** Use off-white text (not pure white) and adequate contrast for readability and accessibility.
- **Endorsement must be consistent.** The GT logo, the "by GT School" line, and cross-links have to appear reliably, or coherence and recall suffer.
- **Then measure.** The strongest evidence is your own: A/B test the two student designs with Austin middle-schoolers (sign-up rate, time-on-page, share rate). Treat the studies above as priors, then confirm with data.
